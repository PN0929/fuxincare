Buying a Home in Taiwan After Retirement: Can You Still Get a Mortgage at 65?

Three steps to budget for a mortgage, prepare overseas income documents and check financing before signing.

If you've spent many years living overseas and are planning to retire in Taiwan, buying a home may seem like a sensible long-term choice. You won't have to worry about renewing a rental agreement, and you'll have a place to settle into retirement.

Finding a property may not be the hardest part. From Fuxin's experience assisting families, mortgage arrangements are often something that needs attention much earlier in the process.

Even with substantial savings and a steady pension, retirees may not qualify for the same mortgage terms as younger, working borrowers. If you find a home and sign a purchase agreement before confirming your financing, you could end up needing much more of your retirement savings than expected.

That's why we recommend reversing the usual order: find out how much you can comfortably borrow before deciding which property to buy.

Here are three practical steps to help you prepare.

Step 1: Start With Your Monthly Payment, Not the Loan-to-Value Ratio

When looking at homes in Taiwan, buyers often ask:

"Can I get a mortgage for 80% of the purchase price?"

But for retirees, an equally important question is:

"How many years will the bank give me to repay the loan?"

Taiwan does not have a blanket rule preventing people aged 65 or older from applying for a mortgage. However, some banks consider both the borrower's age and the repayment period.

For example, a bank may prefer borrowers to finish repaying their mortgages by age 75 or 80. These are possible internal lending criteria, not universal legal limits.

If you're 65 and a bank only offers a 15-year mortgage, your monthly payments could be much higher than those of someone who qualifies for a 30-year mortgage.

How much difference does the repayment period make?

Suppose you borrow NT$6 million at an annual interest rate of 2.5%, with equal monthly payments covering principal and interest.

Mortgage term Approximate monthly payment
30 years NT$23,700
20 years NT$31,800
15 years NT$40,000

Illustrative calculations only. Actual interest rates and repayment terms depend on the bank's approval.

For the same NT$6 million loan, a 15-year mortgage costs approximately NT$16,300 more per month than a 30-year mortgage.

That difference matters when you're living on a relatively fixed retirement income. Money committed to mortgage payments is money you won't have available for daily expenses, healthcare, or future care needs.

A better way to plan your budget

Start by working out how much you could comfortably afford to pay each month while keeping enough money for everyday living and unexpected expenses.

Then ask the bank:

"Based on my age and retirement income, if I want to keep my monthly mortgage payment around this amount, approximately how much could I borrow, and over how many years?"

This gives you a more useful starting point for setting your property budget.

Also, if you already own property or have an existing mortgage in Taiwan, Central Bank lending restrictions may affect how much you can borrow. Don't assume that every property qualifies for 80% financing.

Step 2: Prepare Your Overseas Financial Documents and Compare 2–3 Banks

Here's a situation that can surprise returning retirees: you have a pension, investments, and savings overseas, yet a bank in Taiwan may still consider your mortgage application difficult to approve.

That's because banks don't look only at how much you own. They also want to understand whether you have dependable income to make your monthly repayments.

If you receive a Taiwanese Labor Insurance pension or a government employee pension, the bank may be more familiar with the payment records.

If your retirement income comes from the United States, Canada, or Australia, the bank may need additional documents to verify the source, amount, and consistency of those payments.

Before paying to translate or authenticate every document you have, ask the bank exactly what it requires.

Prepare these three groups of documents

  • Retirement income: Pension statements, recent bank records showing deposits, and evidence of other regular income.
  • Financial assets: Bank account statements from Taiwan and overseas, investment records, and other relevant evidence of your assets.
  • Mortgage application information: Identification documents, your expected home-buying budget, and details of any property or mortgage you already hold in Taiwan.

Take the same information to two or three banks so you can compare their responses.

Focus on three questions.

Will the bank recognize your overseas pension?

Some banks may accept regular pension deposits as evidence of income. Others may require additional documents.

Ask whether English-language documents are acceptable and whether translations, notarization, or authentication by a Taiwan representative office are necessary.

Requirements vary by bank, so there is no reason to arrange document authentication before confirming what your chosen bank needs.

How long can you borrow, and what will your monthly payment be?

Don't compare interest rates alone.

A slightly different interest rate may matter less to your monthly budget than whether the bank offers a 15-year or 20-year repayment period.

Ask each bank to estimate your monthly payment using the mortgage term it would actually consider for your circumstances.

How will the bank assess your overseas assets?

If most of your savings remain overseas, ask whether the bank will accept foreign account statements, whether you need to document the source of the funds, and whether any money must be deposited into a Taiwanese bank account in advance.

Some banks may request a period of account history or require funds to remain on deposit for a certain time. There is no universal three-month or six-month requirement that applies to every bank.

Don't confuse a reverse mortgage with a home purchase mortgage

Some Taiwanese banks offer retirement financing products known as 以房養老, often described in English as reverse mortgages.

These are generally designed for people who already own a property and want to use it as collateral to receive retirement income.

They are different from a mortgage used to buy a home.

When speaking with a bank, make it clear that you're applying for a home purchase mortgage (購屋房貸).

Step 3: Get an Initial Mortgage Assessment Before Signing a Purchase Agreement

This may be the most important step in the entire process.

Imagine you're buying a home for NT$15 million.

You have NT$5 million available and expect to borrow the remaining NT$10 million.

But after signing the purchase agreement, the bank approves only NT$7 million.

You now have an unexpected NT$3 million shortfall.

For someone already retired, finding another NT$3 million could mean using savings originally reserved for living expenses, healthcare, or other plans.

And there's another important point:

A rejected or insufficient mortgage application does not automatically cancel your property purchase agreement.

Whether you can terminate the agreement, must make up the shortfall yourself, or face penalties depends on the contract and the circumstances.

What should you do before signing?

Ask a bank to make an initial assessment based on your age, retirement income, assets, and intended property purchase.

An initial assessment is not a final loan approval. The amount may still change after the bank reviews the specific property, its valuation, ownership records, and other conditions.

Before signing, ask your real estate agent or a licensed land administration agent (地政士) to explain how the purchase agreement handles insufficient mortgage financing.

In particular, confirm what happens if the bank approves less than expected, whether you're required to provide the difference, and whether the agreement allows termination under specified conditions.

These arrangements should be clearly documented in writing. Don't rely on verbal assurances.

If your purchase funds are overseas, plan the transfer early

If your down payment or full purchase funds are held abroad, contact your receiving bank in Taiwan before signing.

Ask what source-of-funds documents are required, whether foreign exchange declarations apply, and how to arrange the transfer in time for the payment deadlines.

If the money comes from a retirement account, such as a Canadian RRSP or TFSA, check the potential tax implications of withdrawals in that country.

Transferring your own savings to Taiwan is not automatically a gift. However, if your children or other family members are providing money for the purchase, separate gift-tax considerations may apply.

The important thing is to resolve these questions before your contractual payment deadlines approach.

After Buying: Don't Put All Your Retirement Savings Into the Property

Your home-buying budget should include more than the purchase price and down payment.

Before and around closing, you may need to pay deed tax, property registration and land administration fees, real estate brokerage fees, moving expenses, and renovation costs.

Once you own the property, you'll also have recurring expenses such as house tax, land value tax, building management fees, and maintenance.

Owner-occupied homes may qualify for preferential property tax treatment, but eligibility requirements apply. If you've recently returned from overseas, check whether your household registration and other circumstances meet the relevant rules.

Most importantly, don't use nearly all your retirement savings to reduce the size of your mortgage. You still need funds for daily living, medical care, and possible assistance as you grow older.

It is also worth thinking about whether the home will remain comfortable ten years from now. An elevator, an accessible bathroom, and convenient access to healthcare and shopping may become increasingly important.

If you're planning to buy a home in Taiwan after retirement, your first step should be to speak with banks using your actual pension and asset records. Once you understand what you can comfortably repay and how many years the bank is willing to lend for, you'll be in a much better position to choose a home without putting unnecessary pressure on your retirement finances.
