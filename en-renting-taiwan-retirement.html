Renting a Home in Taiwan After Retirement: What You Need to Know

A practical guide to agents, age-related rental concerns, rental subsidies, and communicating with landlords.

If you've spent many years living overseas and are planning to retire in Taiwan, finding a place to rent may be one of your first priorities.

But even with a stable pension and sufficient savings, securing a rental home isn't always straightforward. Some landlords hesitate to rent to older adults, while others explicitly state that government rental subsidies are not accepted.

If you've been away from Taiwan for decades, these practices may be unfamiliar.

Will landlords rent to retirees? Should you work with a real estate agent? Can you apply for rental subsidies after moving back?

Here's what you should know before starting your search.

1. Should You Use a Real Estate Agent in Taiwan?

In Taiwan, you can find rental properties directly through platforms such as 591 or work with a real estate agent, commonly called 房仲 (fáng zhòng).

If you're already in Taiwan and familiar with the rental process, searching independently may save you brokerage fees.

However, if you're returning from overseas, cannot easily attend property viewings, or want to know whether a landlord is willing to rent to an older tenant, an agent may make the process easier.

A helpful agent can do more than show properties. They can communicate with landlords in advance, confirm rental conditions, and arrange viewings.

Before choosing an agent, consider asking:

  • Have you helped retirees or older adults find rental homes?
  • Can you confirm whether landlords are open to older tenants before arranging viewings?
  • Can you explain the lease terms and associated fees?
  • How much is your brokerage fee?

Under Taiwan's real estate brokerage fee regulations, the combined fees charged to landlords and tenants for a rental transaction cannot exceed 1.5 months' rent.

This is a legal maximum, not a fixed mandatory fee. Always confirm the actual charges before proceeding.

2. Why Are Some Landlords Reluctant to Rent to Older Adults?

Difficulty finding rental housing is a recognized concern for older adults in Taiwan.

Some landlords worry about medical emergencies involving tenants who live alone, particularly when family members are difficult to contact. Others may be concerned about future care needs or a retiree's ability to pay rent consistently.

However, retirement doesn't necessarily mean financial instability, and being older doesn't mean someone cannot live independently.

If a landlord seems hesitant because of your age, it may help to explain your retirement income, how long you intend to stay, and whether you have an emergency contact.

You don't need to disclose detailed personal medical information to address these concerns.

Would Paying a Year's Rent Upfront Help?

If a landlord is mainly concerned about a tenant's ability to pay, some renters may consider offering six months or even a full year of rent in advance.

While this might address certain concerns, paying a large amount upfront carries significant risks for tenants.

For example, what happens if you discover serious problems with the property after moving in, need to terminate the lease early, or the landlord can no longer fulfill the rental agreement?

Recovering prepaid rent could become complicated.

For this reason, we generally wouldn't recommend paying an entire year's rent simply to persuade a landlord to accept your application.

If both parties agree to advance payment, verify the landlord's authority to rent out the property, sign a written lease, clearly define how unused rent will be refunded in the event of early termination, and keep complete payment records.

3. Can Retirees Apply for Government Rental Subsidies?

Taiwan operates a central government rental subsidy program known as the NT$30 Billion Expanded Rental Subsidy Program (300億元中央擴大租金補貼專案).

2026–2027 policy reminder: This article describes the 2026 program. Taiwan announced its 2027 central rental subsidy program on October 13, 2026. Check the 2027 eligibility rules and application dates before applying.

Eligible tenants can receive government assistance toward their monthly rent.

However, being retired or over the age of 65 does not automatically qualify someone for assistance.

Under the 2026 program, applicants generally must:

  • Hold Republic of China (Taiwan) nationality.
  • Have household registration in Taiwan.
  • Meet household income requirements.
  • Meet applicable residential property ownership restrictions and other eligibility conditions.

For retirees returning after many years overseas, household registration is particularly important.

If you've been living abroad for an extended period, your household registration may have been moved out of Taiwan. You should confirm your current status before assuming you're eligible.

If you receive an overseas pension or other foreign income, check with the relevant authorities about how that income is assessed. Don't assume that income earned or received abroad is excluded from the application.

You can find official program information through the Ministry of the Interior's Real Estate Information Platform.

4. Why Do Some Landlords Refuse Rental Subsidy Applications?

When browsing rental listings in Taiwan, you may come across a phrase such as:

「不接受租補」 — Rental subsidy applications not accepted.

Why would a landlord object to government assistance that helps tenants pay rent?

One common concern involves taxes.

Some landlords worry that a tenant's subsidy application will bring the rental arrangement to the attention of government authorities, potentially affecting rental income reporting or property-related taxes.

Others may simply be unfamiliar with the program or concerned about additional paperwork.

Taiwan has a system known as the Public Welfare Landlord Program (公益出租人).

Landlords who meet the requirements may qualify for tax benefits, including an income tax exemption on up to NT$15,000 in monthly rental income per qualifying property, as well as potentially preferential house and land value tax rates.

Can a Landlord Legally Prevent You From Applying?

No. But that doesn't mean your landlord will never find out.

Under Taiwan's residential lease regulations, landlords cannot include clauses prohibiting tenants from applying for rental subsidies.

Eligible tenants do not need their landlord's prior consent to apply for the central government rental subsidy.

A landlord also cannot arbitrarily increase rent during an existing lease or terminate it early solely because the tenant has applied for assistance.

However, there's an important practical issue that renters should understand.

Your landlord may eventually learn that you've applied for a rental subsidy, even if you didn't tell them beforehand.

Once a subsidy application is approved, relevant rental information is provided to tax authorities in accordance with government procedures. Eligible property owners may also be recognized as public welfare landlords.

A landlord may discover this through public welfare landlord status inquiries, property tax notices, or the following year's income tax filing process.

In other words, not needing your landlord's permission does not guarantee that your application will remain unknown to them.

This is one reason some tenants hesitate to apply, even when they qualify.

They may worry that although the landlord cannot arbitrarily increase rent or terminate the current lease because of the subsidy, the landlord might refuse to renew the agreement when it expires or propose different terms for a future tenancy.

These concerns exist in practice. The Tsuei Ma Ma Foundation (崔媽媽基金會), a Taiwanese housing advocacy organization, has also highlighted issues involving lease renewals and landlord-tenant communication following subsidy applications.

If your landlord has already expressed opposition to rental subsidies, review your lease duration, subsidy eligibility, and legal rights before deciding how to proceed.

You may also seek advice from your local government's land administration department, consumer protection officials, or a tenant rights organization.

If a landlord imposes an unlawful rent increase, attempts an improper early termination, or asks you to sign a clause prohibiting subsidy applications, keep copies of your lease, relevant messages, and other evidence when seeking assistance.

5. How Can You Discuss These Concerns With a Landlord?

If a landlord is hesitant to rent to an older tenant, start by understanding their specific concerns.

For example, you could say:

"We're planning to return to Taiwan for retirement and hope to find a stable, long-term home. We have reliable retirement income. If the landlord is concerned about emergencies or day-to-day property matters, we can arrange for a local contact in Taiwan to help coordinate these issues."

This explains your situation without making unnecessary promises about your health or asking family members to assume legal responsibility.

If the concern is about rental subsidies, first find out whether the landlord is worried about taxes, paperwork, or the property's eligibility.

You might say:

"We're currently checking our eligibility for rental assistance. We also understand that Taiwan offers tax benefits for qualifying public welfare landlords. Would it be possible to review the requirements with the real estate agent so we can understand how they might affect the landlord?"

A conversation like this may help clarify the situation before signing a lease.

However, if the landlord insists that you waive your legal right to apply for rental subsidies or sign a clause prohibiting applications, we would not recommend agreeing simply to secure the property.

6. What Should You Check Before Signing a Lease?

Once you've found a landlord willing to rent to you, take time to review the rental agreement carefully.

Under Taiwan's Rental Housing Market Development and Regulation Act (租賃住宅市場發展及管理條例), the security deposit for a residential lease cannot exceed two months' rent.

Your written lease should also clearly specify:

  • Monthly rent and any building management fees.
  • Utilities and other charges.
  • Responsibility for repairs and maintenance.
  • Lease duration and early termination conditions.
  • Renewal arrangements.

For retirees planning to stay long-term, it may also be worth discussing whether safety improvements, such as installing bathroom grab bars, would be permitted in the future.

Any modifications involving construction or permanent fixtures should be approved by the landlord in advance.

Taiwan's Ministry of the Interior provides standard residential lease templates that can be used for reference. If any contract terms are unclear, ask for clarification before signing.
